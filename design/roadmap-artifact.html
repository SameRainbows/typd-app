Typd · Vision and road

The Road to 10.0

Ten phases from a dictation tool that works to one nobody can copy. Phase N runs from (N−1).1 to N.0, so the version number and the progress are the same thing.

The vision

Voice should be a way to work on text, not just a way to enter it.

Every dictation tool on the market is write-only. You speak, it inserts, the session ends. The moment the output needs changing you are back on the keyboard — which means voice is useless for anything that matters, anything you would revise. Wispr Flow, Typeless, superwhisper, Willow and OpenWhispr all compete on the same axis: words per minute, and how good the cleanup pass is. That axis is saturated, the models underneath it are commodity, and an open-source clone of any of them is a weekend's work.

Typd closes the loop. You speak it, you look at it, you tell it what is wrong, it changes. Produce, inspect, revise — the second half of writing, which nobody in this category has built.

01

The loop is the product. Not the accuracy, not the speed — those are the price of entry. A tool you can talk back to is a different category from a tool you talk into.

02

The people who need this most are not native English speakers. For a native speaker dictation is a 1.3× speedup. For someone thinking in one language and writing in another it is the difference between writing and not writing — and the anxiety it removes is worth more than the seconds. Every incumbent treats this as a "translate" mode bolted on the side, because they were all built by native speakers optimising words per minute.

03

The only moat is that it compounds. Models are commodity and features get copied in a quarter. What cannot be copied is six months of learning how one specific person writes. Every session must leave the app better at you.

The laws

These settle arguments. When a decision is close, the law wins.

  1. 1Your voice never leaves the machine. Recognition is local, permanently, even when that costs accuracy. It is the one claim competitors cannot make honestly, and it is what makes an always-listening session acceptable.
  2. 2The overlay is the entire product surface. If a feature needs a window, it is either the wrong feature or the wrong shape. No dashboard, no settings tour, no stats page anyone visits twice.
  3. 3Never destroy work that cannot be undone. Revision overwrites the user's own writing. Every destructive path needs a way back before it ships.
  4. 4It must compound. A feature that works identically on day 200 and day 1 is a feature a competitor can clone. Prefer the version that accumulates.
  5. 5Nothing ships that is not measured. The bench comes first. "It feels better" is a hypothesis, not a result.
  6. 6Invisible beats clever. Reliability is the feature. A brilliant tool that fails twice a week is uninstalled by the third week.
  7. 7Restraint is the aesthetic. Monochrome, one danger red, no decoration that is not doing work.
  8. 8One gesture, unless the state cannot say it. A new hotkey has to be the only way to express something the current state does not already express. Dictate and revise share a key because the selection already says which is meant. The cost of a chord is not the chord — it is that the user has to hold a decision before acting.
01
0.1 → 1.0

Make the loop real

The revision turn exists and is unverified. Nothing else matters until you can use it for a day without falling back to the keyboard.

0.1

Verify the whole-document guard in Zen, Chrome, VS Code, Word, Notepad, Obsidianneeds you

Highlight one sentence in each and revise it. Only that sentence changes. The debug log names the mechanism used per app.

0.2

Verify the hotkey does not leak to the host applicationneeds you

Press it over a browser, an editor and an elevated terminal. The page must not react. If it leaks, that confirms integrity-level preemption and 0.3 becomes a native hook.

0.3

Undo — Ctrl+Z after a revision restores the original spanneeds you

Revise, undo, compare byte for byte. Must hold in all six applications from 0.1.

0.4

Detect a lost selection between hotkey press and pastedone

Press revise, click elsewhere, finish. It must refuse and say why, not insert at the caret.

0.5

Every failure path has a legible messagedone

Force each: no selection, no microphone, no key, rejected key, timeout, empty completion. Each shows a sentence a non-programmer understands.

0.6

The README describes the app that existsdone

A fresh reader follows it start to finish with no surprises.

0.7

Packaging: the injection helper survives being built into an archiveconfig done

Build, install to a clean path, dictate. The helper spawns from inside the archive.

0.8

Session lifecycle survives abuse2 bugs fixed

Hammer both hotkeys, cancel mid-stream, revise during a dictation, unplug the microphone. No crash, no stuck overlay, no dead click-region.

0.9

First run with no API key is not a dead endneeds you

Launch clean. The message says what to do and where to get it, and dictation still records rather than silently failing.

1.0

Milestone — one working day

Use Typd for a full day of real work. Log every fallback to the keyboard and why. Fewer than three is a pass.

02
1.1 → 2.0

The interface becomes the product

Law 2 says the overlay is the product. Today it is a placeholder with good bones. This is where design stops being a study and lands.

1.1

Review the four design studies and pick a direction

Each study self-assesses honestly. Choose on evidence, not novelty. A rejected direction with a written reason is a result.

1.2

Land the refined pill — proportion, glass, hairline, shadow, radius

Side by side at actual size over busy light and dark backgrounds. Screenshot both, judge cold the next morning.

1.3

Land the voice visualisation

Must read at 36px tall, hold 60fps, and be legible in peripheral vision — you are looking at your document, not at it.

1.4

Rework the state morph so it reads as one object changing

Auto-play the six states at real timings. If any transition reads as a swap rather than a change, it fails.

1.5

Revise mode is visibly distinct from dictate

Someone who has never used it identifies which mode is live from a screenshot. With one hotkey the pill is the only thing that says which mode you are in, and it overwrites their writing — ambiguity here is a data-loss risk.

1.6

Land the mark and wordmark

Judge at 16px in a real taskbar next to real icons. If it needs 32px to work, it is the wrong mark.

1.7

Replace the procedural tray circle with the real mark

Visible and identifiable in light and dark Windows themes at 100% and 150% scaling.

1.8

Audit the sound design

Four cues. Each must be identifiable without looking and must not be annoying on the fortieth use. Test with headphones and with laptop speakers.

1.9

Reduced-motion and high-contrast paths

Force both in Windows settings. Nothing breaks, nothing becomes unreadable, nothing keeps animating.

2.0

Milestone — it looks like a product

Show it to five people cold. Ask what they think it costs. Anything under ten dollars a month means the interface is still under-selling the product.

03
2.1 → 3.0

The session

The architectural bet. Stop treating dictation as one-shot: hold a session open and let revision after revision land on the same text. This is the thing no competitor has, and it only becomes possible once phases 1 and 2 are solid.

2.1

Session state: an open chunk with a tracked region in the target application

The region survives typing above it, scrolling, and window resize. Measure how long tracking holds before it drifts.

2.2

Second and subsequent turns land on the same region

Ten consecutive revisions on one paragraph. The tenth is as accurate as the first — no drift, no accumulated stray whitespace.

2.3

An undo stack across the session

Five revisions, then "go back" three times. Lands exactly on state 2.

2.4

An explicit end-session gesture, distinct from end-turn

Users must never be unsure whether the microphone is live. After ending, speaking near the machine does nothing observable.

2.5

Session-scoped context: the model sees the current draft, not just the span

An instruction referring to something earlier in the draft — "make it match the tone of the first paragraph" — must work.

2.6

Cache the stable draft prefix across turns

Measure turn 1 against turn 5. Turn 5 should be materially faster, or the caching is not working.

2.7

A visible, unmistakable listening indicator for the whole session

A stranger looking at the screen can tell the microphone is open. Non-negotiable given it stays live.

2.8

Insertion versus instruction stays explicit within a session

Fifty utterances that are ambiguous in isolation, all correctly routed.

2.9

Graceful degradation when the target application loses the region

Detect and say so rather than pasting into the wrong place. Test by closing the document mid-session.

3.0

Milestone — write a page by voice alone

Dictate a full page, then revise it to publishable without touching the keyboard. Time it against typing the same page.

04
3.1 → 4.0

It compounds

Law 4. Everything parked was built and tested; this is where it returns — plus the one thing no competitor does, biasing the recogniser before it makes the error rather than repairing after.

3.1

Restore the dictionary

All nine detection tests still pass against current code.

3.2

Restore learn-from-corrections

Fix a word in a pasted result; it is remembered with no dialog. Measure the false-positive rate on ordinary edits — poisoning the dictionary is worse than not learning.

3.3

Feed the dictionary into the recogniser as hotwords, not into the cleanup prompt

Word error on your own voice with and without. Repairing after the fact cannot fix what biasing before prevents; the delta is the whole argument.

3.4

Harvest vocabulary from screen context into a rolling personal lexicon

Names and identifiers accumulate without anyone typing them. Measure lexicon precision — junk in the lexicon is worse than a small lexicon.

3.5

Decay: terms not seen for weeks fall out

Lexicon size stabilises rather than growing forever, and word error does not regress after decay.

3.6

Per-application register learned from evidence, not presets

The same dictation into a code editor and a chat app produces appropriately different output, with no per-app configuration.

3.7

Revision instructions you repeat become cheaper

If you say "tighten this" fifty times, the fiftieth is faster and more predictable than the first.

3.8

Everything learned is inspectable and deletable

Law 3 applied to learning. There must be a way to see and remove what it thinks it knows — without a settings dashboard.

3.9

Learning survives update and reinstall

Export, reinstall, import. Accuracy on your vocabulary is unchanged.

4.0

Milestone — measurably better at you

Re-run word error on your own recordings against the phase-1 baseline. The number must have moved, and the movement must be attributable.

05
4.1 → 5.0

Speak like yourself

The market wedge. This is where the tenfold value is, and where every incumbent is weakest — because none of them were built by someone who needed it.

4.1

Baseline: measure word error on accented English and on Turkish

An honest starting number. Published benchmarks are on native-speaker corpora and will not hold.

4.2

Within-sentence code-switching survives recognition

A bench set of mixed Turkish/English sentences. Competitors detect one language per utterance and destroy these.

4.3

Speak in your first language, write in your second — as a first-class path, not a mode

Meaning preserved, register appropriate, no translationese.

4.4

Fluency repair without meaning drift

Non-native phrasings become native phrasings and a native reader cannot tell. Meaning must be identical — measure drift explicitly; it is the risk.

4.5

Proper nouns and loanwords survive the language boundary

Turkish names in English text keep their diacritics and spelling.

4.6

Revision instructions work in either language

"Bunu kısalt" and "make this shorter" do the same thing to the same span.

4.7

The recogniser learns your accent specifically

Word error on your voice at week 1 against week 6.

4.8

None of this is a setting

No language picker, no mode. It works out of the box or the design is wrong.

4.9

Second-language test with real users

Five non-native speakers, one hour each. The question is not "did it work" but "would you write more because of this".

5.0

Milestone — the wedge is real

A non-native speaker prefers Typd to typing in English. That preference, stated unprompted, is the milestone.

06
5.1 → 6.0

Trust

Aggressive cleanup is only usable if the user believes it did not change their meaning. This is the top churn reason in the category, and nobody addresses it.

5.1

The raw transcript is always recoverable

After any dictation, "what did I actually say" is one gesture away.

5.2

Show what changed in a revision

The touched span is visibly marked as it lands, then fades. Users notice unintended edits they previously missed.

5.3

Never silently return the original

If the model declined to change anything, say so. Silent no-ops read as broken.

5.4

Never silently truncate

Budget exhaustion must be an error, not a half-finished paragraph pasted over a whole one.

5.5

A confidence signal on poor recognition

When confidence is low the interface says so before the text lands, not after.

5.6

The injection boundary stays in the bench, permanently

Both suites' injection cases stay green on every prompt change. Already true; keep it true.

5.7

Nothing leaves the machine that the user did not compose

Audit every outbound request. Screen context is the risk — it can contain anything on screen. Document exactly what is sent.

5.8

A visible, honest privacy statement inside the product

One sentence, checkable. If a future feature breaks it, the sentence changes or the feature does not ship.

5.9

Failure modes are boring

Every error leaves the document exactly as it was. Test by killing the process at each pipeline stage.

6.0

Milestone — trusted with real work

Use it on something that matters without re-reading every word.

07
6.1 → 7.0

Reach

It has to work in the places people actually write. Coverage is unglamorous and it decides whether anyone keeps the app.

6.1

An application coverage matrix, measured not assumed

Word, Outlook, VS Code, Notepad, Obsidian, Slack, Discord, Chrome, Edge, Zen, Teams, Notion — scored.

6.2

Fix or explicitly refuse every red cell

An app that cannot be revised safely must refuse loudly, not silently do the wrong thing.

6.3

Elevated applications

Either a native hook that survives integrity boundaries, or an honest message. Silence is not an option.

6.4

Display scaling at 100%, 125%, 150% and 175%

Correctly sized and positioned at each. Screenshot each.

6.5

Multi-monitor with mixed DPI

The overlay appears on the display holding the focused window.

6.6

Right Alt and other unreachable keys

A native keyboard hook. The hotkey module already exposes the contract for the swap.

6.7

Hotkey conflict detection at launch

Detect a taken binding and name the application holding it, rather than failing silently.

6.8

Cold-start and warm-start timings

The first dictation after launch must not pay the model load.

6.9

Sleep, resume, microphone change, audio device switch

Nothing hangs. The microphone is re-acquired without a restart.

7.0

Milestone — no app-specific excuses

A week of use with no "it doesn't work in X" moment.

08
7.1 → 8.0

Speed

Latency is not the product, but it is the difference between a tool you reach for and one you think about reaching for.

7.1

Instrument the whole pipeline honestly

Per-stage timings on every real session, not just on the bench. Find where the seconds actually go.

7.2

Optimistic paste: raw transcript first, cleaned version swapped in

Measure felt latency separately from actual. If the swap flickers, it fails — high risk, high reward.

7.3

Keep the last utterance's audio in memory

"Try again" re-runs the same audio with a different mode or hint, with no re-speaking. Structurally impossible for streaming-first competitors.

7.4

Cut the revision cold start

The bench measured a 21-second first call against 0.5–8 seconds warm. Find and remove it.

7.5

Edit-operation output for large spans

Emit a targeted replacement, not the whole document. Compare token count and wall time against a full rewrite.

7.6

Streaming reveal tuned to real token arrival

The overlay's reveal matches the stream, not a canned animation.

7.7

Re-evaluate the model choice against the bench

Restraint is the axis that matters, not tokens per second. Diffusion models are worth re-testing when their reliability improves.

7.8

Idle cost

CPU, memory and battery while sitting in the tray all day. A background utility that costs battery gets uninstalled.

7.9

Continuous-listening cost in session mode

Voice-activity gated so recognition only runs on speech. Measure against always-transcribing.

8.0

Milestone — it feels instant

Blind A/B against the phase-1 build. Testers pick the faster one without being told which is which.

09
8.1 → 9.0

Ship it

Everything above is for one person. This is where it becomes something other people can install.

8.1

A signed installer

Installs on a clean Windows machine with no SmartScreen warning.

8.2

Auto-update that never interrupts a session

Update while a session is open.

8.3

Licence chosen, third-party notices carried

Every derived component ships its notice.

8.4

Onboarding that is not a tour

The first successful dictation happens within sixty seconds of install, with no reading. Measure with five people.

8.5

Crash and error telemetry the privacy claim survives

If it cannot be done without sending user content, it does not ship. Law 1.

8.6

Pricing decided

Local inference means near-zero marginal cost. Incumbents charge fifteen dollars a month because they cannot follow you down. Use that deliberately.

8.7

A landing page that shows the loop

The revision loop is the pitch and must be shown, not described. One video, no feature grid.

8.8

A support surface

One channel, answered. Not a help centre nobody reads.

8.9

Uninstall is clean and complete

Everything learned goes with it, on request.

9.0

Milestone — strangers use it

Fifty people who are not you. Watch five of them for an hour.

10
9.1 → 10.0

The moat

Everything above is copyable within a year. This phase is what is not.

9.1

The personal model is a real artifact, not scattered state

Vocabulary, phrasing, register, correction history — one coherent thing.

9.2

It measurably improves month over month

Publish your own word-error curve over six months. A flat line means Law 4 is being violated somewhere.

9.3

The switching cost is real and honest

Six months in, a fresh install of anything else should feel like a downgrade. Test by having someone go back.

9.4

The model is portable and exportable

Law 3. Users own what they taught it — which also makes the claim credible rather than a lock-in threat.

9.5

Revision quality improves from accepted and rejected edits

The instructions you keep and the ones you undo are both signal.

9.6

It learns how you write, not just what words you use

A blind test of whether readers can distinguish Typd-revised text from your own writing.

9.7

A second user on the same machine stays separate

Profiles do not bleed.

9.8

The compounding story is legible on day one

New users must understand that it gets better, or they will not stay long enough for it to.

9.9

Re-run the full bench suite against the phase-1 baseline

Every number better, and every regression explained.

10.0

Milestone — uncopyable

A competitor could clone every feature in a quarter and still not have this.

What this road refuses

Written down so it stays refused.

  • ×No settings dashboard. Law 2. Configuration goes in the environment or is learned. The moment there is a preferences window, the thesis is dead.
  • ×No cloud recognition. Law 1. Not as an option, not as a "faster mode".
  • ×No agent, no tool-calling, no "ask anything". It is built, it works, and it is still the wrong product. Typd is a text tool.
  • ×No feature that works identically on day 1 and day 200. Law 4.
  • ×No chat interface. The target application is the interface.
  • ×No mobile. The whole product is a desktop overlay pasting into desktop applications.
  • ×No second hotkey without a fight. Law 8. If a feature seems to need one, the first question is what on screen could have told us instead.
