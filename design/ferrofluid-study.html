Typd — visualisation study 01

Ferrofluid in a 56 by 18 pixel hole.

The idea: your voice is the magnetic field. Silence is a calm bead of liquid; speech pulls it apart, spikes it, clusters it. Three ways of doing that, plus the eleven-bar meter we ship today, all painted from one animation loop at the size that actually decides it. Note the size. The brief said the meter slot was 90 px wide. Measured live in the browser it is 56 — 130, less the 2 px border, less 12 of padding, less 48 of controls, less 12 of gap. Nearly forty per cent narrower than assumed, and the assumption was doing real work: at 90 px two of these three look like different designs than they do here.

A — Bead

—

Metaballs on the centreline. One parent bead and two pairs of satellites. The inner pair is placed so the iso-surface can never quite break — it necks and holds — while the outer pair is thrown far enough to pinch clean off, so a loud phrase is one stretched body with two droplets flung off it.
Good at the resting state, which is the best frame on this page: one wet black bead with a hard specular, sitting still. Nothing else here makes silence look deliberate rather than idle. It is also the only design that never borrows the grammar of a chart. Bad at filling the slot when loud — the composition is a mass and two 6 px dots with real gaps between them, and at DPR 1 those dots lose their edges. It reads quieter than B at every level above about 0.5.

B — Spikes

—

Rosensweig instability. A pool with a fixed spike wavelength — in the real fluid the field changes spike height, never spacing — and a critical threshold below which the surface stays flat. Neighbouring spikes are diffusively coupled so the row rises as one surface.
Good at almost everything, which was not the prediction. Six spikes in 56 px was supposed to be the eleven-bar meter wearing pointed hats; it is not, because it is one filled body with a continuous outline rather than eleven separate marks, and the eye reads the silhouette. It is the most legible thing on this page at actual size and the only one that looks like the photograph everybody pictures. Bad at the company it keeps: a filled mass anchored to a baseline with a jagged top is the exact grammar of a spectrum analyser, so it is simultaneously the most convincing ferrofluid and the most convincing audio visualiser. It is also the only asymmetric element in a symmetrical pill.

C — Skin

—

Pre-instability corrugation. One closed body that never changes topology. Voice drives how far it spreads and how agitated its surface is: three travelling capillary waves at different wavelengths and speeds, interfering so the ripple never repeats.
Good at surviving: nothing has to resolve, so it holds up at DPR 1 and at 1.25, which is where most Windows laptops actually live — this machine reports 1.25. The gloss has more continuous surface to sit on than in either of the others. Bad at being ferrofluid. Without spiking or splitting it is a very good blob, and at actual size the corrugation flattens into a slightly lumpy worm. A viewer who was not told would not name the material, and probably would not notice the ripple at all.

Incumbent — 11 bars

—

What ships today, copied out of overlay.js unchanged so the comparison is fair: eleven rounded bars, centre-weighted, each with a phase offset, snapped to whole device pixels.
Good at being unambiguous at any DPR and reading as "listening" in the first 100 ms. Every edge is axis-aligned so it is the only one here that is genuinely crisp. Bad at having any character at all — it is the same meter as everyone else's, and it says nothing about the product beyond "audio is arriving".

Timed with performance.now() around the step-and-paint of each renderer, smoothed with a 0.05 EMA, so the numbers settle after a second or two. Compositing is excluded, which for a 56 × 18 canvas is not the part to worry about. Measured in Electron 41 on this machine — a Windows laptop reporting DPR 1.25 — with a live speech envelope running, all four visualisers and all eight canvases painting at once: A 0.04–0.10 ms, B 0.036–0.046 ms, C 0.016–0.042 ms, the incumbent bars 0.005–0.018 ms, and 0.27–0.40 ms for everything on the page. The frame rate never left 120.

So the performance question is not interesting: against a 16.7 ms budget the most expensive of these costs about six tenths of one per cent, and the shipping case is a quarter of what this page does. The only honest observation in the table is relative — the metaball solver is roughly eight times the cost of the bars it would replace, and eight times almost nothing is still almost nothing. This should not be a factor in the decision, and the numbers are here mainly so that nobody has to guess.

Verdict

Yes, it works — but not for the reason the concept promises, and the version I expected to fail is the one that reads best.

The prediction going in was that ferrofluid is a large-format idea: that everything making the material recognisable is high-frequency detail — the hexagonal spike lattice, the specular on each cone, a spike splitting in two as the field rises — and that all of it would be sub-pixel at this size. Half of that is right. The half that is wrong is B, and it is worth being specific about why the prediction failed, because the reasoning was plausible and the reasoning was bad. Six spikes across 56 px really is the same spatial frequency as eleven bars across 56 px. But eleven bars are eleven separate marks and six spikes are one filled body with one outline, and the eye reads silhouettes, not frequencies. B is the most legible element on this page at actual size. I would not have believed that from the arithmetic.

I would still ship A. Not because it is more legible — it is not, above about half volume B beats it clearly — but because of what the two are made of. B's loud state is a filled mass anchored to a baseline with a jagged top edge, which is the grammar of a spectrum analyser down to the last detail; it is at once the most convincing ferrofluid and the most convincing audio visualiser, and the product does not want the second thing. A never touches that grammar. And A owns the state the overlay is actually in most of the time: a still, wet, black bead is a thing at rest, whereas eleven short bars are a graph reading nearly zero. The README's line is that an overlay asking to be looked at has already failed, and between a quiet bead and a serrated mountain range there is only one answer consistent with that.

C is the safe answer and I do not recommend it. It is the most robust — it holds at DPR 1, it never flickers, the gloss has the most surface to sit on — but it bought that by trading away the concept. Ship C and in three months nobody will remember it was meant to be ferrofluid; it will be the blob. Prefer A, which is recognisably an idea, and accept that its outer droplets are soft.

Two things fight A and both are real. First, ferrofluid is black, and on the dark theme it cannot be. A near-white fluid on a near-black pill reads as milk or mercury. Gloss mitigates it — the dark-theme body drops to a grey so a bright crown has somewhere to sit, which states "lit from above" from the other side — but the material identity is a light-theme privilege and this study should not pretend otherwise. Second, at DPR 1 and 1.25 the droplets lose their edges. Turn on 4× shows real pixels and drop to DPR 1: next to the incumbent bars, which are axis-aligned and snapped to whole device pixels and consequently razor sharp, all three fluids are visibly soft. That is not a bug to fix. Curved outlines at 18 px are soft, and anyone choosing a fluid is choosing to give up the crispest thing in the pill.

Three tuning failures are recorded in the comments rather than quietly fixed, because each one is a fact about the size rather than about the code. Metaballs merge at roughly 2.8 radii, not at contact, so six satellites across 28 px of half-slot never separated and the loud state was a potato. Letting all four satellites detach then gave five circles in a row, which is not a liquid but an ellipsis, and read as a loading indicator. And the gloss gradient, anchored to the top of the slot rather than to the top of the liquid, put its brightest stop in empty air and made every body read as flat grey. All three looked fine in the code and wrong on the glass.

One measurement worth carrying back: the slot is 56 px, not 90. If ferrofluid is wanted badly, the cheapest intervention is not a better solver but a wider slot — shrink the two 24 px controls to 20, or widen the pill by 16, and B stops being a spectrum analyser because there is room for the pool to sit well inside its own margins instead of spanning wall to wall.

Everything here is synthetic. The envelope is a phrase/pause state machine with a 4–6.4 Hz syllable train, per-phrase loudness, occasional stressed syllables and a floor of room tone, which is a better test surface than the two multiplied sines in overlay.js because it produces the awkward cases — a long hold, a hard stop mid-phrase — that break a visualiser. No microphone is requested and nothing leaves the page.
