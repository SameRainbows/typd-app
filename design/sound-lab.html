The one thing that is a number

Loss is how much level the cue gives up when everything below ~500 Hz is taken away, which is roughly what a laptop speaker does. Under 3 dB the cue is the same sound on both devices. Over 8 dB it is a different sound, and the version you tuned on headphones is not the version most people hear. Peak is headroom against full scale at unity master; length is time to −40 dB, and it is the number that predicts annoyance far better than loudness does.

The assessment

What is wrong with the current cues

The intent written at the top of sound.js is right and the execution does not deliver it. Six specific faults, in the order they cost the most.

1. Half the energy is under the laptop speaker's floor

start is built on 392 Hz and cancel falls to 311 Hz. Typical laptop and ultrabook drivers are 30–45 mm and roll off hard below about 500 Hz. The table above measures it: start loses about 9.7 dB, cancel about 9.4, and both are flagged as failing, while every cue in all three replacement sets holds within about 2 dB. commit thins by 3.4 dB. done, built higher at 659 and 988 Hz, survives — which is itself the argument, because it shows the problem is the choice of register and nothing else. Cancel is the worst of it: the part that carries its meaning is the fall, and the fall is the part that disappears. This is not "quieter on speakers". It is a different sound on speakers, which means every judgement made on headphones is void.

2. The taming filter does nothing

The master lowpass sits at 2400 Hz with the comment that it makes the tones "sit behind whatever else is playing". Every voice in the set is a sine. A sine has no partials, so a 2.4 kHz lowpass applied to a 392 Hz sine removes nothing at all; the filter is inert and has been since it was written. Worse, the belief behind it is backwards: a sustained pure tone is the single most detectable signal in noise there is — it is what audiometry uses precisely because it punches through everything. What makes a cue sit behind music is not darkness, it is brevity. Short transients are masked by ongoing sound; long tones are not.

3. start and commit are the same sound

Both are a two-note rising exponential glide with a faint octave above, both about 130 ms, both sine, both at full level. They differ only by being transposed up a fourth. Nobody can name an absolute transposition without a reference tone, and these two fire several seconds apart with speech in between, so there is no reference. Blind identifiability — the first clause of the bar — fails here outright, and it fails on the two cues that fire on every single session.

4. done is a notification chime

Two pure sines, 659 then 988, a rising perfect fifth 65 ms apart. That is the canonical message-received shape, and the brief rules it out by name. It is also hummable, and hummable is the property that turns a cue into an earworm somewhere around the twentieth repetition. Melody is the enemy of repetition tolerance.

5. The levels are balanced by gain, not by loudness

start and commit default to level 1; done runs at 0.55 and 0.45. But at these very low playback levels the ear is around 10–12 dB less sensitive at 400 Hz than at 1 kHz, and the speaker takes another 10 dB off the same region. So the cue written loudest arrives quietest and the cue written quietest arrives loudest — the exact inverse of the intent, and invisible from the source.

6. There is no error cue at all

The roadmap names four cues: start, commit, done, error. The file ships start, commit, done, cancel and a parked learned. session:error in overlay.js plays nothing. This is the worst gap in the set, because failure is the one moment the user has already looked away — they pressed the key, spoke, and went back to reading. A silent failure is discovered when they eventually look at the pill, or when they notice the paragraph they expected never arrived. Every other cue is a convenience; this one is the only cue that is load-bearing.

What changed, and why

Four rules, applied to all three new sets.

  1. Nothing depends on bass. Every fundamental sits between about 740 Hz and 1.8 kHz. That band is above the small-speaker knee and below the 4–5 kHz region where the ear is most easily irritated. A cue that measures under 3 dB of loss is the same object in both ears and both speakers, and can therefore be judged once.
  2. Shorter, not quieter. Switch the table to Length to −40 dB. The single-gesture cues drop from 109–136 ms to between 36 and 95 ms; commit goes from 118 ms to 44. The two-strike cues stay around 140 ms and always will, because the gap between the strikes is the message. This is the lever that makes a sound sit behind music, and it is also the lever on annoyance: what irritates on the fortieth hearing is how long the sound occupies attention, not its peak.
  3. Differentiate by gesture, never by transposition. One strike versus one damped strike versus two strikes versus a falling strike versus two identical dull strikes. Rhythm and envelope are identifiable blind with no reference; pitch offsets are not. This is what fixes fault 3, and it fixes it without adding a note.
  4. Object, not tone. Each voice carries a second partial at 2.756× the fundamental — the first bar mode of a free-free bar, the ratio that gives a marimba or an anvil its characteristic non-piano colour — which decays about three times faster than the fundamental, plus a 5–8 ms band-passed noise transient for the strike itself. That inharmonicity and that fast-dying partial are what read as "something was struck" rather than "an oscillator was switched on", and they are exactly what the current file set out to achieve with a lowpass that could not do it.

The three sets

  • Detent — the Braun reference taken literally. Almost all of the energy is a high-Q band-passed noise burst, which rings at a pitch without ever being a note. Character: a well-machined switch. Risk: at Q values this high it starts to sound like a synthesised click rather than a mechanism, and it has the least room to express difference between six cues.
  • Breath — no oscillators anywhere, filtered noise only. A page turning, air moving, a fingertip on fabric. It is the least intrusive set by a distance. Risk, and it is a real one: it may be too invisible. Turn on the laptop simulation, start some music, and see whether you can still tell done from commit.
  • Struck — the middle path, and the one to ship. Enough tonal body to be identifiable through a laptop speaker with music playing, enough inharmonicity and noise to never read as a beep, and the widest usable space between six gestures.

How Struck separates the cues

  • start — one strike, softish attack, ~115 ms tail, tiny downward glide. An object set down. It opens because it rings.
  • commit — the same pitch as start, harder attack, damped to ~50 ms. Identical note, half the length: it reads as closing because the tail is cut off, not because it went up. Fixing fault 3 without spending a new pitch is the single change I would defend hardest.
  • done — two strikes, 58 ms apart, second one quieter and a whole tone up. A whole tone is the smallest step that still reads as motion and the largest one that does not read as a tune.
  • cancel — one strike falling 740 → 555 Hz through a 1.9 kHz lowpass. Dull, dropped, over. The fall is still there, but it now starts above the speaker knee instead of ending below it.
  • error — two identical dull strikes at 784 Hz, 115 ms apart. Repetition reads as refusal without any interval at all.
  • learned — one very short, very quiet high tick. A footnote.

Does revise need its own done?

Yes — and only done.

Roadmap 1.5 already says revise must be visibly distinct from dictate, on the grounds that it overwrites the user's own writing and ambiguity there is a data-loss risk. That argument transfers to sound and gets stronger, because sound is the channel that is live precisely when the user is not looking — which is the moment a destructive action most needs marking.

Of the five cues, done is the only one whose meaning actually differs between the modes. "Text was added where your cursor was" and "text you wrote has been replaced" are different events, and they are separated by several seconds of model latency from the keypress that chose the mode — long enough for attention to have moved on. Law 3 says every destructive path needs a way back; the way back is Ctrl+Z, and it is only reachable by someone who knows something was overwritten. One second of audio is the cheapest possible way to tell them.

The honest argument against is that five cues is already near the limit of what becomes reflex rather than vocabulary, and a sixth risks tipping the whole set from "felt" to "learned". So the differentiation is deliberately minimal: revise done is the same two strikes as done, same timbre, same length, same loudness — with the contour and the emphasis inverted. It descends and lands on the louder note. Not a new sound; the same sound backwards. The set still has five gestures.

start should not differ by mode, and I built a version that did before cutting it. The user pressed a specific key one frame ago. A distinct revise-start spends a sixth sound telling them something they already know, and it makes the two most frequent cues in the product four instead of two.

What I would ship

Struck, with the error cue wired up and a revise-specific done.

It is the only one of the three that holds up on a laptop speaker with music playing and still reads as designed rather than as a system beep. Detent is more distinctive per cue and has less headroom across six of them; Breath is the most pleasant and I do not trust it to survive being ignored. Ship Struck, keep Breath in this file, and revisit it if Struck turns out to be the thing people notice.

This recommendation is a hypothesis with an instrument attached, which is the most law 5 allows on a question this subjective. Two of the three clauses of the acceptance bar can be checked here — small-speaker survival is in the table above as decibels, and the fortieth use is one button away. The third, blind identifiability, needs a second person: play the six cues in random order with the screen turned away and have them name each one. Under five out of six and the set is not finished, whatever the numbers say.

What failed

Documented because a rejected direction with a reason is a result, and because several of these are the obvious first idea.

  1. Square and sawtooth for the mechanical click. The intuitive way to make a click sound mechanical is a harmonically rich waveform. It is wrong here: the odd-harmonic stack lands energy at 3f and 5f, straight into the 2–4 kHz region where small speakers have a resonant peak, so the very device that swallows the low end amplifies the buzz. It reads cheap. Band-passed noise gives the same mechanism without the harmonic series, which is why Detent is built on noise. Set any voice to square in the editor with the laptop simulation on and it is immediate.
  2. A falling minor third for error. The universal "uh-oh". Rejected on three counts: it is the most clichéd interval in interface audio, it is melodic and therefore memorable and therefore irritating by the fortieth use, and it belongs to the operating system's vocabulary rather than the product's. Two identical dull strikes convey refusal through repetition and carry no tune at all.
  3. Making the cues quieter to make them less annoying. The instinct, and it backfires twice. A quiet long tone is more annoying than a loud short one, because irritation tracks how long the sound holds attention rather than its peak. And dropping the master gain pushes the cue under the laptop speaker's usable floor once any other audio is playing, at which point the user raises the system volume and everything else on the machine gets louder instead. Shortening is the lever; quieting is not.
  4. A downward filter sweep on start in Breath. Built it, cut it. A bandpass sweeping down over noise is a whoosh, and a whoosh is a game-UI signature — the exact register the brief rules out. The sweep survives only on cancel, where something genuinely is leaving and the gesture is honest.
  5. A three-note done. Three notes is a phrase, a phrase is a tune, and it pushed the cue past 200 ms — still sounding while the user is already reading the text that landed. Two notes is the ceiling.
  6. Matching cues by equal gain. The first pass gave every cue the same level and it was audibly lopsided, for the same equal-loudness reason that fault 5 describes. There is no clean fix in code — the per-cue levels here are hand-trimmed toward a rough 40-phon compensation and they are the parameter most likely to still be wrong. Trim them by ear before shipping; that is what the sliders are for.
  7. A separate start for revise mode. Covered above. Built, then cut for costing a sound to say something already known.
  8. Keeping the 2400 Hz master lowpass. Inert on the old set and actively harmful on the new ones — it would eat the noise transient at 2.2–2.6 kHz that supplies the entire "struck" character. Replaced with a gentler 10 kHz lowpass, which takes the fizz off the noise without touching the strike, plus a limiter so that hammering the hotkey (roadmap 0.8) stacks voices without clipping.

One thing this page cannot do

It cannot hear. Every number above is computed and every adjective is a prediction. The frequency choices, the durations and the gestural separation are defensible from physics and from the failure modes named in the bar; the balance between the voices inside each cue is not, and it is the part most likely to need an hour of dragging sliders. Do that hour before wiring any of this into sound.js.
